P2 Statistical and epidemiological concepts

Preliminary learning — statistics and epidemiology

TipLearning objectives

By the end of this chapter, participants should be able to:

  1. Define and calculate the principal measures of disease frequency and severity: risk, attack rate, incidence rate, prevalence and case fatality ratio.
  2. Describe the characteristics of surveillance data that affect their analysis: case definitions, under-ascertainment, reporting delays and the type of date.
  3. Identify the probability distributions most frequently used in infectious disease modelling, and evaluate them in R.
  4. Calculate and interpret confidence intervals for proportions and rates.
  5. Explain the concept of likelihood and of the maximum likelihood estimate.
  6. Fit and interpret a Poisson regression model for count data.

P2.1 Measures of disease frequency

Measure Definition Example
Risk (cumulative incidence) number of new cases during a period ÷ population at risk at the start of the period proportion of children infected during a measles outbreak
Attack rate risk during an outbreak (a proportion, despite its name) 512 of 763 boys confined to bed: 67%
Secondary attack rate new cases among contacts of a case ÷ number of contacts proportion of household contacts infected
Incidence rate number of new cases ÷ person-time at risk 150 tuberculosis cases per 100,000 person-years
Prevalence number of existing cases ÷ population at a given time proportion of children with malaria parasitaemia in a survey
Case fatality ratio (CFR) deaths among cases ÷ number of cases proportion of confirmed Ebola cases who die
Infection fatality ratio (IFR) deaths ÷ number of infections (including undetected infections) proportion of all SARS-CoV-2 infections, estimated from a serological survey, that resulted in death
Mortality rate deaths ÷ person-time in the whole population 45 malaria deaths per 100,000 person-years among children under five years

Prevalence, incidence and duration are related: in a stable situation, and when prevalence is low,

\[ \text{prevalence} \approx \text{incidence rate} \times \text{mean duration of disease}. \]

This relationship reappears in compartmental models, where the number of individuals in a compartment equals the inflow multiplied by the mean time spent in it.

# Attack rate in the 1978 boarding school influenza outbreak
attack_rate <- 512 / 763
attack_rate
## [1] 0.6710354
# Prevalence from incidence and duration: 150 cases per 100,000 per year,
# mean duration of untreated tuberculosis about 2 years
150 / 1e5 * 2 * 1e5     # prevalent cases per 100,000
## [1] 300

The CFR is a property of detected cases and depends on the case definition and on the intensity of testing: if mild cases are not detected, the CFR overestimates the risk of death per infection. During an outbreak, the CFR calculated from reported cases is also biased by the delay between onset and death (course book: Severity: the case fatality ratio).

P2.2 Characteristics of surveillance data

Models are frequently fitted to routine surveillance data, which have several characteristics that must be taken into account (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention 2012):

  • Case definitions distinguish suspected, probable and confirmed cases; changes in the case definition change the reported numbers.
  • Under-ascertainment: only a proportion of infections results in symptoms, care seeking, testing and notification. This proportion may change over time, for instance when testing capacity increases.
  • Reporting delays: cases are reported days or weeks after symptom onset, so that the most recent counts are incomplete (course book: Reporting delays and nowcasting).
  • Type of date: date of infection, symptom onset, consultation, sampling, laboratory confirmation or report. Each date implies a different delay with respect to infection.
  • Aggregation: Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR) systems and DHIS2 typically report weekly counts by district; line lists contain one row per case.

The epidemic curve — the number of cases by date of onset — is the basic description of an outbreak and the principal input for transmission models. Descriptive epidemiology by time, place and person is treated in detail by Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (2012), lessons 1 and 4–6.

P2.3 Probability distributions

Probability distributions describe the variability of counts and durations. The following are used repeatedly in this book:

Distribution Type Mean Variance Typical use in modelling
Binomial(\(n\), \(p\)) counts from 0 to \(n\) \(np\) \(np(1-p)\) number infected among \(n\) contacts; number seropositive in a survey
Poisson(\(\lambda\)) counts ≥ 0 \(\lambda\) \(\lambda\) number of cases per day or week
Negative binomial(\(\mu\), \(k\)) counts ≥ 0 \(\mu\) \(\mu + \mu^2/k\) overdispersed counts; number of secondary cases (superspreading)
Exponential(rate \(r\)) durations > 0 \(1/r\) \(1/r^2\) time spent in a compartment of an ODE model
Gamma(shape \(a\), rate \(b\)) durations > 0 \(a/b\) \(a/b^2\) incubation period, serial and generation intervals, reporting delays
Lognormal(\(\mu\), \(\sigma\)) durations > 0 \(e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}\) \((e^{\sigma^2} - 1)\, e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}\) incubation period
Normal(\(\mu\), \(\sigma\)) continuous \(\mu\) \(\sigma^2\) approximations; prior distributions
Beta(\(a\), \(b\)) proportions in (0, 1) \(\dfrac{a}{a+b}\) \(\dfrac{ab}{(a+b)^2(a+b+1)}\) prior distributions for proportions

In R, each distribution has four functions: d... (probability or density), p... (cumulative distribution function), q... (quantile function) and r... (random draws), e.g. dpois(), ppois(), qpois(), rpois().

dbinom(3, size = 20, prob = 0.1)       # P(3 of 20 contacts infected)
## [1] 0.1901199
ppois(10, lambda = 6)                  # P(at most 10 cases in a week with mean 6)
## [1] 0.9573791
qgamma(0.95, shape = 4, rate = 0.4)    # 95th percentile of a gamma delay with mean 10 days
## [1] 19.38414
pexp(5, rate = 1 / 5)                  # P(leaving a compartment within its mean duration)
## [1] 0.6321206

The last result shows an important property of compartmental models based on ordinary differential equations: the time spent in a compartment follows an exponential distribution, so that 63% of individuals leave the compartment before its mean duration and a substantial proportion remains much longer. Real delay distributions are usually less dispersed and are better described by a gamma or lognormal distribution (Figure 1).

The negative binomial distribution includes a dispersion parameter \(k\); as \(k\) increases, it approaches the Poisson distribution. Small values of \(k\) correspond to counts that are more variable than expected under a Poisson model (overdispersion), which is common in surveillance data and in the number of secondary cases per case (course book: Stochastic models).

P2.4 Estimation and confidence intervals

A sample statistic, such as a proportion, estimates an unknown population value with sampling error. A 95% confidence interval is constructed such that, in repeated samples, 95% of such intervals would include the true value.

# Seroprevalence: 34 positive of 120 tested (exact binomial confidence interval)
binom.test(34, 120)$conf.int
## [1] 0.2048557 0.3728205
## attr(,"conf.level")
## [1] 0.95
# Incidence rate: 45 cases in 30,000 person-years (exact Poisson confidence interval)
poisson.test(45, T = 30000)$conf.int * 1e5    # per 100,000 person-years
## [1] 109.4110 200.7118
## attr(,"conf.level")
## [1] 0.95

Confidence intervals reflect random sampling error only; they do not account for systematic errors such as selection bias, misclassification or under-ascertainment.

P2.5 Likelihood

The likelihood expresses how probable the observed data are for a given value of a parameter. For 34 positives among 120 tested, the likelihood of a seroprevalence \(p\) is the binomial probability \(L(p) = \binom{120}{34} p^{34} (1-p)^{86}\). The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) is the value of \(p\) that maximises \(L(p)\); here it equals the observed proportion \(34/120\). In practice the logarithm of the likelihood is used, as it is numerically more convenient.

log_lik <- function(p) dbinom(34, size = 120, prob = p, log = TRUE)
mle <- optimize(log_lik, interval = c(0.001, 0.999), maximum = TRUE)$maximum
c(mle = mle, observed = 34 / 120)
##       mle  observed 
## 0.2833345 0.2833333

The same principle is used to estimate the parameters of transmission models: the model predicts the expected number of cases, and the parameters that maximise the likelihood of the observed counts are selected (course book: Linking models to data). Values of the parameter for which the log-likelihood is within 1.92 units of its maximum form an approximate 95% confidence interval (Figure 2). In Bayesian inference, the likelihood is combined with a prior distribution (course book: Bayesian model fitting).

P2.6 Regression models for count data

Poisson regression models the logarithm of the expected count as a linear function of explanatory variables. An offset equal to the logarithm of the population (or person-time) converts counts into rates; exponentiated coefficients are rate ratios.

set.seed(1)
districts <- tibble(
  district = paste("District", 1:12),
  population = round(runif(12, 50000, 300000)),
  coverage = round(runif(12, 0.5, 0.95), 2)            # vaccination coverage
) |>
  mutate(cases = rpois(12, lambda = population * 2e-4 * exp(-3 * (coverage - 0.7))))

fit <- glm(cases ~ coverage + offset(log(population)), family = poisson, data = districts)
summary(fit)$coefficients
##              Estimate Std. Error    z value     Pr(>|z|)
## (Intercept) -6.566844  0.3575204 -18.367747 2.380913e-75
## coverage    -2.773596  0.4703854  -5.896434 3.714418e-09
exp(coef(fit)[["coverage"]] * 0.1)     # rate ratio per 10-percentage-point increase in coverage
## [1] 0.7577819

Poisson regression on time is also used to estimate the growth rate of an epidemic from early case counts (course book: Estimation of the reproduction number from the growth rate). When counts are overdispersed, the standard errors of a Poisson model are too small; a quasi-Poisson (family = quasipoisson) or negative binomial model (MASS::glm.nb()) should then be used.

TipExercise
  1. In a district of 80,000 inhabitants, 240 cholera cases and 6 deaths are reported during an outbreak lasting 10 weeks. Calculate the attack rate per 1,000 inhabitants and the CFR, with 95% confidence intervals.
  2. Using rnbinom(), simulate 1,000 weekly counts with mean 10 and dispersion \(k\) = 2, and compare their variance with that of 1,000 Poisson counts with mean 10.
  3. A gamma distribution has mean 9 days and standard deviation 5 days. Calculate its shape and rate parameters, and the proportion of delays longer than 21 days. (Hint: shape = mean²/SD², rate = mean/SD².)

## [1] 0.95

## [1] 0.009228601 0.053617475
## attr(,"conf.level")
## [1] 0.95
